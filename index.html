HALLOWEEN • 3e • A2/B1

From turnips to pumpkins

Discover how a tradition travels and changes.

A · More than one beginning

A glowing pumpkin on a doorstep may look timeless, but Halloween has changed over centuries. One influence is Samhain, a Gaelic festival associated with the end of the harvest and the arrival of winter. Irish folklore linked this season with spirits and the dead. Some customs were intended to protect homes and families. These stories tell us about people’s beliefs, not about the existence of ghosts.

B · Two histories meet

Christian celebrations also helped shape the festival. All Saints’ Day falls on 1 November, and the evening before it was called All Hallows’ Eve. The name gradually became Halloween. Local stories, seasonal customs and religious celebrations developed alongside one another. It would be misleading to claim that every modern Halloween custom comes directly from Samhain.

C · An object changes its meaning

In Ireland, people carved faces into turnips and placed lights inside them. Such lanterns were linked with stories about spirits, including the legend of Stingy Jack, a man condemned to wander with a light. Irish and Scottish immigrants helped carry Halloween customs to North America in the nineteenth century. There, pumpkins became popular lanterns because they were available and easier to carve. A familiar object therefore tells a story of migration and adaptation.

D · Rewards, neighbours and shops

In guising, visitors wore disguises and performed a song, a joke or a small act to receive food or money. Guising is one possible influence on modern trick-or-treating, whose origins are debated. Trick-or-treating became increasingly popular in the United States in the mid-twentieth century. Today, neighbours may hand out sweets and enjoy meeting families, while shops sell costumes and decorations. Halloween can create a sense of community, but it is also a commercial event. Traditions can keep familiar features while gaining new meanings.
